# Report Template

The output shape. Follow the structure; write the prose like a human, not a form.

Save to `~/research/people/<firstname-lastname>.md`.

---

## Structure

```markdown
# <Full Name> — Life Story

**Researched:** <date> · **Sources:** <N> · **Footprint:** rich | moderate | thin | near-zero
**LinkedIn:** <url>

> ⚠️ Include this line ONLY when it applies:
> **Minimal public footprint.** Little about this person exists in public sources. Everything below
> is what is actually verifiable; the Gaps section says what I could not establish and why.

## The short version

Two paragraphs. Who they are now, and the one sentence that explains how they got there. A reader
who stops here should still have learned something real.

Inline citations are optional here — it is a recap, and brackets make it unreadable. Two rules make
that licence safe, and both get broken in practice:

1. **Every fact here must appear, cited, in the body below.**
2. **Nothing here may sound surer than the body does.** A claim tiered *Self-reported* downstream
   must read as *"by his own account…"* here, not as fact. This is the sneaky one: the report stays
   technically honest while the summary — the part most people actually read — quietly upgrades a
   claim the report itself doubts.

Check both explicitly before saving.

## Origins

Where the story starts: upbringing, education, the first choices — but only what is public. If the
record begins at their first job, then the story begins at their first job. Say so and move on;
do not reach for a childhood you do not have.

## <Narrative sections — name them for the chapters of the actual life>

Not "Career History". Use headings that carry meaning: "The Goldman years", "Leaving the money",
"Building Ovenbird". Three to five sections, chronological, each covering an era and — crucially —
**the seam that ends it**. Why they left. What broke. What they were chasing.

Every factual sentence carries a citation [1] or a visible hedge ("no public source explains…",
"the timeline suggests, though nothing confirms…").

Quote them directly wherever you have their words. Their voice is the point.

## Where they are now

Current role, current preoccupations, what they've said recently about what's next.

## The through-line

The thread that runs through it — the preoccupation, the recurring bet, the thing they keep
returning to. Argue it from the evidence. If the evidence doesn't support one, say the record shows
no clear arc, and say what would need to be known to find one. A false arc is worse than none.

# Appendix

## Timeline

| Period | What | Confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2011–2015 | BA Economics, University of Michigan | Self-reported | [1] |
| 2015–2019 | Goldman Sachs — Analyst → VP | Confirmed | [1][4] |
| 2019–2020 | *No role on profile. Unexplained by any public source.* | — | — |
| 2020– | Founder, Ovenbird Bakery | Confirmed | [2][7] |

Show the gaps as rows. A gap is data.

## Confidence table

Every load-bearing claim, and how solid it is. This is what makes the report auditable — a reader
should be able to look up any sentence in the narrative and find out how much to trust it.

| Claim | Confidence | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Left Goldman in 2019 after making VP | Confirmed | Two independent sources [4][7] |
| Left because of burnout | Probable | Their own account on the *Rise & Grind* podcast [2] — single source, self-reported |
| Family had a bakery background | Inferred | They referred to "my grandmother's ovens" [2]; nothing confirms a family business |
| The 2019–2020 gap | Unverified | No public source addresses it |

Confidence levels: **Confirmed** (independent source, page opened) · **Probable** (single credible
source) · **Self-reported** (only the subject says it) · **Inferred** (your reasoning, marked in the
narrative too) · **Unverified** (uncorroborated, kept because it's load-bearing).

## Claims that did not survive checking

Include this section whenever something in circulation turned out not to hold. It is frequently the
most useful part of the report, because it is the part that stops the reader repeating a falsehood
they would otherwise have had no reason to doubt.

| Claim in circulation | Where it comes from | What checking found |
|---|---|---|
| "Arrested at the protest" | Wikipedia; repeated in most summaries | The article Wikipedia cites reports 31 arrests and **does not name her** [4]. She may have been among the unnamed; nothing establishes it. Independent monitors confirm only that she was *questioned* [5][6]. |
| "CMO at Northwind" | Aggregator sites, her own bio | Independent sources title her Head of Partnerships [8][9]. The CMO was someone else. |
| "Raised 22M, PE-backed" | Her company's own team page | The figure appears in no source outside that page [10]. Contemporary press describes a single private backer. |
| "Father was a pilot" | A search snippet | Appears on no page that could be opened. Excluded as probable conflation with another person. |

State what checking found, not a verdict you can't support. "The source does not establish this" is
different from "this is false", and the difference is the whole point.

## Gaps — what I could not establish

The honest list. Name the seam questions that stayed unanswered, and say *why*: no public source, a
name collision that couldn't be broken, a platform that blocked the fetch, a footprint in a language
you couldn't search. Also name the sources you tried and could not reach.

This section is not an apology. It is the part of the report that tells the reader how much of the
picture is missing — and a report that hides its blind spots can't be trusted by anyone except the
person who wrote it.

## Sources

Numbered, matching the inline citations. Title, publisher, date, URL. Mark each as **[primary]**
(the subject's own words), **[press]**, **[company]** (the employer speaking about its own person —
not a neutral witness), or **[record]**.

1. LinkedIn profile, as provided by user, retrieved <date> — [primary]
2. "Ep. 112: Sarah Chen on leaving finance", *Rise & Grind*, Mar 2022, <url> — [primary]
3. …
```

## Notes on writing it

**Headings are an argument.** "Career History" tells the reader nothing. "Leaving the money" tells
them a life turned. Name the chapters for what happened in them.

**The short version is the part most people will actually read.** Make it carry weight.

**Don't let citation discipline decay.** It always holds in the first section and slips by the
fourth, where the prose gets confident and the sources thin out. That's precisely where invention
creeps in. Watch for it.

**Never state a birth year, address, family member's name, or anything about health, finances,
religion or politics unless the subject made it public themselves** — and even then, only if it
bears on the story.
